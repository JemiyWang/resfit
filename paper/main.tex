\title{SHORE-RL: Shortening the Horizon for Long-Horizon Residual\\
Reinforcement Learning}

\author{Anonymous Submission}
\affiliations{}

\begin{abstract}
Behavior-cloned manipulation policies, including modern vision-language-action (VLA)
models, are strong but bounded by the quality of the demonstrations they imitate. Freezing such a cloned
base policy and learning a small additive residual with off-policy RL is an effective way to
improve beyond this ceiling. On long-horizon manipulation, however, the
evaluated RL-finetuning methods fail to sustain improvement and finish below
the frozen base policy. \textbf{SHORE-RL} addresses this late-training collapse
by reducing the long horizon to a sequence of short ones, so that each residual
correction is conditioned on a nearby, self-derived target rather than only on
a long, sparsely rewarded trajectory. SHORE-RL is a base-policy-agnostic
plug-in: a navigator extracts latent waypoints (\emph{where to go}) from a
goal-conditioned value learned on frozen base-policy features, without
object-pose or goal-distance inputs, while a separate critic-reward path
provides dense progress feedback (\emph{how well the policy is doing}). In
simulation, SHORE-RL achieves high and sustained success rates on four
long-horizon DexMimicGen tasks, while action-space residual, noise-space,
imitation-bootstrapped, and full-policy RL baselines collapse below their
frozen base policies. The same plug-in also improves a frozen $\pi_0$ policy
on LIBERO. On a real robot, residual training in world-model imagination
improves success over the frozen base policy on three long-horizon tasks.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
Behavior-cloned (BC) manipulation policies provide a strong control prior, but they
inherit the errors and coverage limits of their demonstrations~\cite{ross2011dagger,mandlekar2021matters}. Residual
reinforcement learning (RL) offers a parameter-efficient route beyond this ceiling:
it freezes the pretrained policy and learns only a bounded additive action
correction~\cite{johannink2019residual}. Recent frozen-policy adaptation methods show
that restricting the trainable component can make online finetuning substantially
more sample efficient~\cite{ball2023efficient,resfit2025residual}. This restriction,
however, acts on the \emph{adaptation space}, not its temporal span: ResFit
still optimizes every local correction from the task-level return over the remaining
episode.

We study late-training collapse in long-horizon residual finetuning. Across the
evaluated methods, some policies improve transiently whereas others degrade
earlier, but their long-horizon performance eventually falls below the same
frozen base reference. In a controlled horizon comparison, ResFit remains
stable on the shorter-horizon task but collapses on long-horizon, sparsely
rewarded manipulation. This pattern identifies extended temporal composition
as the central empirical factor in our setting. Sparse delayed
feedback~\cite{arjona2019rudder} also provides a mechanism-level explanation:
each correction receives weak local evidence of whether it helps, while critic
error and residual drift can accumulate over the remaining trajectory. We use
this analysis to motivate the design, not to claim that any one mechanism is
the independently established cause of collapse.

Our central question is whether residual finetuning can remain stable on
long-horizon, sparse-reward tasks by shortening the effective objective horizon
faced by each correction. We separate two roles. On the actor path, a nearby
latent waypoint conditions the residual policy and localizes its target. On the
critic path, an independent progress signal supplies dense shaped rewards; it
neither consumes the waypoint nor evaluates waypoint progress. Learning curves
identify late-training collapse, and final-window success rate summarizes
whether an improvement is sustained.

We introduce \textbf{SHORE-RL}, a horizon-shortening framework for frozen-policy
residual adaptation. A navigator learned from demonstrations proposes latent waypoints
from frozen base-policy features, without simulator-only object poses, goal
coordinates, or geometric distance-to-goal signals. These waypoints condition only the
residual actor. A separate path supplies dense rewards only to the TD3 critic:
the main simulation study uses a hand-designed per-stage bonus, and a
self-derived alternative uses differences of a learned single-state potential.
The goal-conditioned waypoint value and learned potential remain functionally
independent. We finally study real-robot learning through a learned dynamics
model as an extension of the same residual objective.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{A long-horizon failure pattern and mechanism analysis.} We show
empirically that existing RL-finetuning methods fall below their frozen bases
on long-horizon tasks while ResFit remains stable on a shorter-horizon control.
We connect this pattern to sparse delayed credit and the accumulation of critic
error and residual drift, and quantify sustained performance with final-window
success rate.
\item \textbf{Waypoint-conditioned residual RL.} To our knowledge, SHORE-RL is
the first RL-finetuning method to use latent waypoints to shorten the objective
horizon of a frozen-base residual. It is a base-policy-agnostic plug-in that
requires neither simulator-only object poses, goal coordinates, nor geometric
distance-to-goal inputs.
\item \textbf{Evidence across tasks and base policies.} We evaluate sustained
performance against action-space, noise-space, imitation-bootstrapped, and
full-policy baselines on long-horizon manipulation, test the plug-in with ACT
and $\pi_0$, compare a stage bonus with a stage-detector-free learned
potential, and extend the residual objective to real-robot imagination.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}
% =====================================================================
\paragraph{RL Finetuning of Imitation Policies.}
Behavior cloning provides a strong initialization for robot control but remains limited
by demonstration quality and coverage~\cite{ross2011dagger,mandlekar2021matters}. Online RL can improve this initialization, and
prior-data methods improve sample efficiency by mixing demonstrations with newly
collected transitions~\cite{ball2023efficient,nakamoto2023calql,zhang2023pex,li2023explore}. IBRL retains the imitation policy as an
action proposal and uses learned values to choose between imitation and RL
actions~\cite{hu2023imitation}. These methods primarily address initialization, data
reuse, or how strongly the learned policy remains anchored to imitation. Our focus is
sustained final-window performance when temporal composition is extended.

\paragraph{Residual and Latent-Space Policy Adaptation.}
Residual RL freezes a controller and learns an additive action
correction~\cite{johannink2019residual,yuan2025policydecorator}. ResFit applies this parameterization to
behavior-cloned manipulation policies and combines it with sample-efficient off-policy
learning from prior data~\cite{resfit2025residual,ball2023efficient}. DSRL instead
optimizes the initial noise of a frozen diffusion or flow policy, moving adaptation
into a latent sampling space~\cite{wagenmaker2025dsrl}. These approaches differ in the
\emph{space} in which adaptation occurs and in how it is constrained around the base~\cite{ren2025dppo}.
They do not provide each residual correction with a temporally local objective.

\paragraph{Temporal Abstraction and Progress Shaping.}
Temporal abstraction decomposes a long task into intermediate
goals~\cite{nachum2018hiro,fang2022ptp,shi2023skimo,zheng2024prise}. HIQL, for
example, learns a high-level latent subgoal together with a learned low-level
policy that reaches it~\cite{park2023hiql}. Existing RL-finetuning methods for
frozen imitation policies instead adapt actions, latent noise, or action
selection without using waypoints to shorten the residual objective horizon.
SHORE-RL fills this gap by conditioning only the frozen-base residual on a
value-ranked future feature. Progress shaping is complementary:
transition-level feedback may come from a stage bonus or from potential
differences~\cite{ng1999policy,devlin2012dynamic}.

\paragraph{World-Model Robot Learning as an Extension.}
Learned dynamics models provide policy-learning experience when direct robot
interaction is expensive~\cite{hafner2020dreamer,janner2019mbpo,hansen2024tdmpc2,mazzaglia2023choreographer}.
RISE uses an action-conditioned video model for robot policy
improvement~\cite{yang2026rise,escontrela2023viper}. We similarly use learned
dynamics as a training environment, while retaining the same frozen-base
residual objective.

\section{Preliminaries}
% =====================================================================
\paragraph{Problem setting.}
We consider an episodic partially observable control problem with latent Markov state
$x_t$, observation $o_t$, action $a_t$, sparse task reward $r_t$, discount
$\gamma\in[0,1)$, and horizon $T$. A policy may depend on the available observation
history $o_{\leq t}$; we therefore reserve $x_t$ for theoretical state-based statements
and do not identify it with a learned visual representation. The objective is the
expected discounted task return. We use \emph{long horizon} to describe tasks
that require extended temporal composition before sparse terminal feedback.

\paragraph{Frozen-policy residual adaptation.}
Let $\pi_b$ be a behavior-cloned policy whose parameters remain fixed during RL. From
$o_{\leq t}$, the base proposes an action or flattened action chunk
$a_t^b\sim\pi_b(\cdot\mid o_{\leq t})$. The ResFit actor samples a bounded
correction
$a_t^{\mathrm{res}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{res}}(\cdot\mid
o_{\leq t},a_t^b)$, with $a_t^{\mathrm{res}}\in[-1,1]^{d_a}$, and
the environment executes
\begin{equation}
a_t=a_t^b+s_a a_t^{\mathrm{res}},
\label{eq:residual-action}
\end{equation}
where $s_a>0$ bounds deviation from the base. The base policy remains frozen; only the
adaptation modules defined in \S4 are trainable. Following prior residual
finetuning~\cite{resfit2025residual}, our implementation uses TD3
critics~\cite{fujimoto2018addressing} and mixes online transitions with demonstrations
in the RLPD regime~\cite{ball2023efficient}. ResFit conditions on the current
observation and base action, whereas SHORE-RL additionally conditions its actor on a
latent waypoint.

\paragraph{Goal-conditioned values.}
Let $e_t=\psi(o_{\leq t})$ denote a feature produced by the frozen base-policy encoder;
$e_t$ is a learned representation and is distinct from the environment state $x_t$.
Let $g$ denote the task-goal representation. A goal-conditioned value
$V_{\mathrm{gc}}(e,g)$ ranks features by their estimated progress toward $g$.
Following HIQL~\cite{park2023hiql}, a high-level policy can be extracted
by advantage-weighted regression over future features in demonstration trajectories.
SHORE-RL uses this standard construction to obtain a latent waypoint, but does not use
the HIQL low-level policy: the waypoint only conditions the residual actor. The
estimator, future-state sampling, and online update objectives are specified in the
method rather than treated as background.

\section{Method: SHORE-RL}
% =====================================================================
Figure~\ref{fig:framework} summarizes SHORE-RL, a plug-in that wraps a frozen
base policy through two independent learning paths. The actor path combines a
bounded residual policy (\S\ref{sec:hier}) with a base-feature navigator
(\S\ref{sec:value}) whose latent waypoint conditions each correction. The
critic path supplies dense progress rewards (\S\ref{sec:pot}): our main
simulation study uses a hand-designed per-stage bonus, and a separate
self-derived variant uses potential differences. \S\ref{sec:wm} changes the
source of transitions and rewards for a learned-dynamics extension while
retaining the same frozen-base residual architecture.

\subsection{Horizon-Shortening Residual Policy}
\label{sec:hier}
At each step, the frozen base encoder produces
$e_t=\psi(o_{\leq t})$, and a navigator initialized offline and jointly
finetuned online (\S\ref{sec:value}) emits a latent waypoint
$z_t=\pi_h(e_t,g)\in\mathbb{R}^{10}$. The residual actor conditions its bounded
correction on this waypoint:
$a_t^{\mathrm{res}}\sim\pi_{\mathrm{res}}(\cdot\mid
o_{\leq t},a_t^b,z_t)$.
It receives no simulator-only object poses, goal coordinates, or geometric
distance-to-goal inputs. The residual is trained with the off-policy objective
of \S3; the base action stream is executed open-loop within each action chunk.
The waypoint enters only the residual actor; the reward-shaping path of
\S\ref{sec:pot} neither consumes nor evaluates it.

\subsection{Base-Policy-Derived Goal-Conditioned Value}
\label{sec:value}
The waypoint $z_t$ above is produced by a goal-conditioned value and its navigator
$\pi_h$. The value's state representation is the frozen encoder feature of the base policy:
$e_t=\psi(o_{\leq t})=\mathrm{Enc}_{\pi_b}(o_{\leq t})$, using the
ACT~\cite{zhao2023learning} or
$\pi_0$~\cite{black2024pi0} encoder. Crucially, $\psi$ is available from images alone at
deployment and receives no simulator-only object poses, goal coordinates, or geometric
distance-to-goal signals. We train this goal-conditioned
value offline on demonstrations with a goal-reaching internal reward ($1$ at the final
step, $0$ otherwise) and the HIQL objective, caching $\psi$ with a signature-checked cache
for reuse. This yields the goal-conditioned value $V_{\mathrm{gc}}(e,g)$ and
the navigator $\pi_h$ that emits $z_t$. During residual RL, we jointly
finetune $V_{\mathrm{gc}}$ and $\pi_h$ on a mixture of online residual-policy
and offline demonstration transitions, while keeping the base policy and
encoder frozen. This aligns the waypoint generator with the online state
distribution. $V_{\mathrm{gc}}$ supports waypoint selection only and never
constructs the critic reward.

\subsection{Dense Critic Reward Shaping}
\label{sec:pot}
A sparse terminal reward gives the residual little per-step progress feedback.
We therefore consider two dense critic-reward forms. In our main simulation
experiments, a fixed task-specific detector supplies a hand-designed per-stage
bonus $b_t^{\mathrm{stage}}$, and TD3 uses
$r_t^{\mathrm{stage}}=r_t+b_t^{\mathrm{stage}}$. The bonus exposes only the
coarse phase reached, not object poses, goal distances, or corrective actions.
Its limitation is the need for a predefined decomposition and phase detector.

The stage-detector-free alternative is potential based. Let $u$ denote the
residual-training gradient-update index. We construct~\cite{ng1999policy,devlin2012dynamic}
\begin{equation}
r_{t,u}^{\Phi}
=
r_t+\gamma\Phi_u(e_{t+1})-\Phi_u(e_t),
\label{eq:pbs}
\end{equation}
where $e_t=\psi(o_{\leq t})$, $c>0$ is a reward scale, and
$\Phi_u(e)=cV_{\mathrm p}^{(u)}(e)$. The single-state value
$V_{\mathrm p}$ is trained with the same frozen features, demonstrations, and
recipe as $V_{\mathrm{gc}}$, but it is a distinct network with the goal
marginalized out. Thus $V_{\mathrm{gc}}$ selects waypoints, whereas
$V_{\mathrm p}$ constructs critic rewards. $V_{\mathrm p}$ is updated during
residual RL rather than kept fixed, so we do not invoke a fixed-potential
policy-invariance guarantee for the full training process. Both dense reward
forms enter only the TD3 critic target and never condition the actor.

\subsection{Extension: Residual Training with Learned Dynamics}
\label{sec:wm}
Direct residual RL is interaction intensive on hardware, so we train from
imagined transitions generated by a learned dynamics model. The model $D$
predicts the next $H$ multi-view frames from an observation history and action
chunk,
$\hat{o}_{t+1:t+H}=D(o_{\leq t},a_{t:t+H-1})$. We finetune an
action-conditioned multi-view video-diffusion checkpoint~\cite{yang2026rise}
on pooled expert demonstrations and deployed-base rollouts from all real-robot
tasks, then freeze it. During residual learning, real starting states seed
short rollouts under $a=a_b+s_a a_{\mathrm{res}}$; imagined frames are
re-encoded by the frozen base encoder and stored in an imagination replay
buffer together with real transitions. Rollouts recurse at most twice to limit
model-error accumulation~\cite{janner2019mbpo}. Because imagined rollouts have
no environment success signal, the critic uses the stage-detector-free
potential difference from $V_{\mathrm p}$ as its surrogate reward. The
dynamics model changes only the source of training transitions and reward; it
is not used at deployment and is not a second core SHORE-RL component.

\paragraph{Tasks and Evaluation.}
We evaluate SHORE-RL on four long-horizon dual-arm dexterous manipulation tasks from
DexMimicGen~\cite{jia2024dexmimicgen,mandlekar2023mimicgen}
(\textsc{ThreePieceAssembly}, \textsc{Pouring}, \textsc{LiftTray}, \textsc{Threading}),
with the shorter-horizon DexMimicGen \textsc{CanSort} as a same-benchmark control and a
single-arm extension on LIBERO~\cite{liu2023libero} (Fig.~\ref{fig:simtask}). Base policies are
ACT~\cite{zhao2023learning} (primary) and $\pi_0$~\cite{black2024pi0}. Each evaluation runs $50$ episodes (standard error $\approx 0.06$ near
$p{=}0.8$). Our primary summary metric, \emph{final-window success}, is
the mean success over the last $20\%$ of evaluations within the stated
comparison budget, reported with seed variation. The learning curves in Fig.~\ref{fig:main} show whether this
performance is sustained or undergoes late-training collapse.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
We compare SHORE-RL against four external references and our component
ablations under matched evaluation conditions, including the number of
evaluation episodes, replan cadence, success criterion, frozen base, and
training budget. Method-specific anchoring controls---the action scale for our
residual, the noise scale for DSRL, and the exploration scale for IBRL---are
matched as closely as their parameterizations allow. \emph{(i)~BC base}: the
frozen base policy with no RL, which determines whether adaptation sustains an
improvement. \emph{(ii)~ResFit}~\cite{resfit2025residual}: the published
demo-anchored residual parameterization and our primary prior method.
\emph{(iii)~DSRL}~\cite{wagenmaker2025dsrl}: noise-space RL on the same base,
which tests whether changing the adaptation space prevents late degradation.
\emph{(iv)~IBRL}~\cite{hu2023imitation}: a BC-bootstrapped off-policy method
that selects between base and RL actions with a $Q$-argmax. \emph{(v)~Full-policy
off-policy RL} (IQL~\cite{kostrikov2022iql}): finetuning the whole policy
without a frozen base, included as a non-residual control. Its late degradation
is consistent with the importance of retaining a frozen policy anchor, but does
not by itself establish a unique cause of instability.

\paragraph{Sustained Long-Horizon Performance.}
Figure~\ref{fig:main} shows learning curves for four long-horizon tasks and the
shorter-horizon \textsc{CanSort} control, while Table~\ref{tab:main}
summarizes final-window success on the long-horizon tasks. SHORE-RL maintains
high success throughout training on \textsc{Pouring} and \textsc{LiftTray}
and sustains clear improvements on \textsc{ThreePieceAssembly} and
\textsc{Threading}. ResFit, DSRL, IBRL, and full-policy IQL instead finish
below their frozen-base references on the four long-horizon tasks. Their
different adaptation spaces and anchoring mechanisms therefore do not by
themselves prevent low late-training performance. In contrast, ResFit remains
near-optimal on the shorter-horizon \textsc{CanSort} control. Together, the
curves and final-window results support temporal composition as the central
empirical factor and show that SHORE-RL sustains the improvement over its
frozen base across all four long-horizon tasks.

\paragraph{Base Generality: ACT and \textbf{$\pi_0$.}}
We test the framework with two frozen base-policy classes, ACT and $\pi_0$,
using only the observations and features exposed by each base. Beyond the
ACT-based dual-arm tasks, we port the value, waypoint, and residual modules to a
served $\pi_0$ VLA on image-only LIBERO-10~\cite{liu2023libero}, with robot
proprioception and two camera views but no simulator state. On
\textsc{Task~8}, the demo-anchored module reaches near-perfect final-window
success, compared with approximately $0.81$ for DSRL and $0.76$ for the frozen
base under the matched query-20 protocol; the standard query-5 base is $0.38$.
Because DSRL uses a separate codebase and evaluation harness, that cross-method
comparison is indicative rather than strictly controlled
(Fig.~\ref{fig:libero}). The within-harness frozen-base comparison supports the
applicability of the horizon-shortened residual design to both tested base
classes without implying universality beyond ACT and $\pi_0$.

\paragraph{Component Ablations.}
Figure~\ref{fig:ablbars} summarizes matched component variants at a common
$400$k cutoff on two long-horizon tasks. The effects are task dependent and
reveal interactions among waypoint conditioning, behavior anchoring, and critic
shaping. On \textsc{ThreePieceAssembly}, SHORE-RL reaches $.65$, whereas every

\paragraph{Reward Form: Stage Bonus vs.\ Self-Derived Potential.}
The main long-horizon study uses a hand-designed per-stage signal, whose
limitation is the need for a task-specific phase detector and a predefined
decomposition. We test whether the detector can be removed while holding the
rest of SHORE-RL fixed. The self-derived alternative uses the
separate single-state value $V_{\mathrm p}$ trained from the same frozen
features and demonstrations, and changes only the critic reward source.
Figure~\ref{fig:stagevspot} gives the controlled comparison on the two
stage-structured tasks. Final-window success with $V_{\mathrm p}$ is
indistinguishable from stage shaping on \textsc{Threading} ($.52$ vs.\ $.52$)
and is numerically higher on \textsc{ThreePieceAssembly} ($.70$ vs.\ $.66$).
Both panels use three seeds. On \textsc{Threading}, both shaped arms remain only slightly above
the frozen base ($\approx\!.47$), so this task is a weak discriminator;
\textsc{ThreePieceAssembly} provides the stronger evidence. These results
support removing the hand-written stage detector in the tested settings; they
do not require or imply that $V_{\mathrm p}$ participates in waypoint
selection.

\paragraph{Platform.}
Our platform is a full-body robot; we command its two $7$-DoF arms, each
fitted with a parallel gripper, in a $16$-D joint-and-gripper action space. Perception is
image-only: one head-mounted camera and two wrist-mounted RGB cameras stream
$848{\times}480$ video at $30$\,Hz---the same three views consumed by the base policies and
the dynamics model. Each base policy is a served
$\pi_{0.5}$-style VLA~\cite{sima2026kai0} that outputs $50$-step action chunks, executed
open-loop within each chunk at a $30$\,Hz low-level control rate (\S\ref{sec:hier}). At deployment the robot runs only the
task's frozen base plus the small residual and waypoint heads on its features; the dynamics model
is a training-time device and is never executed at inference time.

\paragraph{Tasks.}
We evaluate on three tabletop tasks---\textsc{pick\_paper\_roll},
\textsc{build\_block}, and \textsc{pick\_cup}
(Fig.~\ref{fig:realtask})---covering precision picking of a rolling object,
assembly-style block stacking, and cup grasping and placement, with bimanual
coordination throughout.

\paragraph{Pipeline.}
For each task, we collect approximately $1{,}000$ success-only teleoperated
episodes of $800$--$2{,}500$ steps at $30$\,Hz and finetune a separate
$\pi_{0.5}$ VLA, which is then frozen. We deploy each base on the physical
robot to collect additional rollouts containing its observed failures. A
single dynamics model $D$ is jointly full-finetuned on the pooled expert and
base-rollout data from all three tasks and then frozen (\S\ref{sec:wm}). Each
task-specific SHORE-RL residual is trained inside $D$ using the self-derived
potential difference, without object poses, goal distances, or external frame
scorers. We evaluate the resulting residual on the physical robot with $50$
trials per cell from a fixed initial-state set.

\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
SHORE-RL recasts residual BC finetuning as horizon reduction: latent waypoints
derived from frozen base-policy features provide nearby actor targets, while a
separate critic-reward path supplies the progress feedback absent from sparse
terminal rewards. Across four long-horizon dual-arm tasks, SHORE-RL achieves
high and sustained final-window success, whereas the evaluated existing
RL-finetuning baselines collapse below their frozen base policies. The main
simulation study uses a hand-designed per-stage bonus. On two
stage-structured tasks, potential differences from a self-derived single-state
value match or exceed this bonus, providing a path that removes the
task-specific stage detector.
